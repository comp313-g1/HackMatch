## Overview

Hackathons bring people together to build working ideas within a limited period. Their value includes technical experimentation and new professional relationships, but team formation requires people to reconcile different skills, ambitions, and availability. Research on hackathons identifies a tradeoff between advancing technical work and developing social ties; assembling a team is therefore part of the collaboration challenge, rather than simply an administrative step [1]. HackMatch is a proposed website for students and early-career developers who want to find teammates before a hackathon. It connects people seeking a team with Participants recruiting for specific openings, around a shared, verified record of the event.

The wider developer ecosystem gives this focused opportunity context: GitHub's 2025 Octoverse report describes more than 180 million developers on its platform [2]. That platform-reported figure is not HackMatch's addressable market or evidence of demand for another service. The investment case is more specific: support a bounded prototype and pilot that test whether structured recruitment helps participants reach confirmed teams with less uncertainty. HackMatch combines event verification, visible openings, candidate suggestions, applications, invitations, and a final roster. The semester deliverable will demonstrate that complete workflow; commercial adoption, willingness to pay, and operational costs remain questions to validate before a broader launch.

## Executive Summary

### Problem Statement

HackMatch targets students and early-career developers who want to join hackathons but need collaborators beyond their existing contacts, alongside Participants recruiting for incomplete teams. Their problem is translating interest into a clear, mutually accepted team commitment: which event is being discussed, which skills are needed, who is still available, and whether an opening remains unfilled. When this information is spread across introductions, messages, and manually maintained lists, users must reconcile it themselves. Research supports examining this coordination problem, although it does not establish its prevalence in HackMatch's intended pilot population. Trainer et al. found that team-formation strategies involve tradeoffs between technical progress and social relationships [1]. Schulten et al.'s study of one online hackathon found that the official communication platform supported event organization and contact with officials, while participants also used other tools [3]. These findings do not imply that chat platforms are ineffective; they suggest that communication and recruitment state are different needs. For a prospective participant, unclear availability can mean pursuing an already-filled opening; for a team owner, it can mean planning around someone who has committed elsewhere. HackMatch will test whether a shared recruitment workflow reduces such uncertainty, without assuming that it guarantees stronger teams or eliminates the need for conversation.

### Proposed Solution

HackMatch proposes a structured path from discovering a hackathon to confirming a team. Participants create verified accounts and profiles describing their skills and goals, identify the events for which they are available, and browse open team listings. A Participant who creates a team becomes its Team Lead for that team and receives rule-based candidate suggestions. Participants can apply to openings, while Team Leads can send invitations; both routes lead to the same membership checks and visible decision states. For example, a student interested in interface design can find a team seeking that contribution, review its objective, and apply without first knowing its owner. A missing event can be submitted by any verified Participant, but it remains private until an Administrator checks and approves it. Recruitment then occurs around one public, verified HackMatch event record. Once members are confirmed, the Team Lead can lock a valid roster. This approach is intended to reduce repeated clarification about openings and commitments while allowing people to choose their collaborators. It complements existing communication tools, rather than attempting to replace them. The MVP includes required email-based login verification and limited administration; advanced matching, extensive coordination tools, and event operations are excluded from the semester commitment.

## Project Overview

### Description

HackMatch is a responsive website organized around Participants, verified hackathon records, and teams with specific recruitment needs. An unauthenticated Visitor can discover approved events and inspect anonymized team vacancies before creating an account. Public registration creates a Participant account, coded `PART`; it never provisions an Administrator. A Participant can register with email verification or use the agreed Google/GitHub sign-in paths, and must complete the required email login code before receiving an authenticated session. Profiles contain self-declared skills, a primary contribution area, portfolio links, and institution information where applicable. They help people describe what they can contribute without treating a school affiliation, link, or skill claim as independently certified. Authenticated Participants can inspect permitted profile fields; Visitors cannot inspect participant identities or portfolio/contact details. Optional shared contact handles become visible only to confirmed teammates. The product's personas are hypothetical design tools, not evidence that interviews or usability studies have already validated these choices.

Event discovery is the common starting point for recruitment. A verified, signed-in Participant may submit a missing hackathon even before creating a team. The workflow is **verified PART submits → private pending review → ADMIN approval → public verified HackMatch event record → team formation enabled**. The pending record is visible only to its submitter and Administrators. Review checks the official source, event edition, dates, and published team limits; a valid web address alone does not establish authenticity. Exact duplicates are detected using normalized event links and edition information, while possible similarities receive review rather than automatic merging. Administrators may correct, approve, reject, or create records directly under the same verification standard. Invalid or misleading submissions receive feedback; unsafe published links and recruitment can be disabled while historical rosters are preserved. Verification establishes a usable HackMatch record, not organizer endorsement or attendee admission. The research planning kit's attention to different event formats and goals reinforces why HackMatch records event-specific rules instead of assuming all hackathons operate identically [4].

For each approved event, a Participant can declare Looking-for-Team status, available commitment hours, and an objective such as learning or project completion. A Participant who creates a recruitment listing becomes that team's owner and first confirmed member. Team Lead is a contextual permission attached to ownership, not a separate account type: someone may lead a team for one hackathon and participate normally in another. The listing expresses the project concept, desired contributions, and open slots within the event's official limits. Ordinary Participants browse and filter those openings; they do not receive algorithmic team recommendations in the MVP. Team Leads receive candidate suggestions based on explicit rules comparing vacancies with available Participants' declared skills, goals, and commitment. Suggestions explain relevant factors and remain advisory. A displayed score does not certify ability or predict project success. Profile accuracy, incomplete information, and the proposed scoring parameters therefore remain practical limitations requiring evaluation, rather than reasons to describe matching as proven intelligence.

Recruitment supports both applications from Participants and invitations from Team Leads. An invitation is an offer, not a reserved place; an application is an expression of interest, not membership. Invitation acceptance and application approval must recheck eligibility and capacity together so simultaneous decisions cannot overfill a slot or confirm the same person on two teams for one hackathon. Confirmation occurs at team creation, accepted invitation, or approved application, before roster locking. Successful placement deactivates the relevant Looking-for-Team status and cancels competing pending requests for that event. Basic alerts explain the current status within the ordinary workflow. Before lock, a non-owner may leave and an owner may remove a member with a reason; the owner can disband the team rather than transfer ownership. Locking requires an event-compliant roster and closes changes under the agreed lifecycle. This provides a clear commitment record, but does not register that roster with an external organizer. Export and a dedicated team coordination workspace are later enhancements.

Platform Administrators, coded `ADMIN`, maintain event records, manage accounts and authorized deletion requests, and perform basic content moderation through a limited case workflow. They can initiate cases directly; participant reporting and a searchable audit interface are post-MVP. Basic administrative action records remain part of the MVP. All primary login routes, including Google/GitHub sign-in, require email OTP: codes expire after ten minutes, and five incorrect attempts trigger a fifteen-minute account login lock. This is the agreed course policy, not a claim of compliance with NIST's authentication assurance levels: NIST excludes email as an out-of-band authenticator [5]. The website must support desktop, tablet/iPad, and phone portrait/landscape use, with accessibility assessed against the specified WCAG 2.1 AA target [6]. These are acceptance commitments, not demonstrated performance or conformance. Organizers remain external sources of event information; professors and evaluators assess the academic deliverables. HackMatch does not operate ticketing, payments, judging, attendee registration, or event scheduling.

### Value Proposition

**A clearer path from interest to commitment** is HackMatch's primary value proposition. Team formation has both social and technical objectives: research describes tradeoffs between building relationships and advancing work, while the revised planning kit treats event design as dependent on purpose and participants [1], [4]. HackMatch applies that evidence through explicit openings, goals, and recruitment states, rather than assuming a single ideal team composition. A Participant should be able to distinguish a pending application from a confirmed place; a Team Lead should see which vacancy is still available. Discord already offers searchable, tagged forum posts [7], so the proposed benefit is not simply making messages searchable. It is maintaining commitments and capacity consistently across the recruitment process. That could reduce repeated clarification and abandoned approaches to filled teams. The size of any improvement remains unknown. A proposed pilot should compare time to confirmed placement and unresolved recruitment requests with the participants' existing workflow, reporting sample size and unsuccessful journeys as well as successful ones.

**More relevant discovery** means exposing useful differences among potential teammates without assuming that a long profile or fashionable technology predicts success. Stack Overflow's 2025 survey covers over 49,000 responses across 177 countries and a wide range of technologies [8]. JetBrains' 2025 study includes 24,534 cleaned responses and explicitly balances its sample across factors including programming language, while acknowledging remaining selection bias [9]. These industry reports establish a varied developer context; neither is a survey of demand for HackMatch. The product implication is to allow people to describe contribution areas and event-specific goals instead of relying on a generic developer label. A Team Lead seeking a designer can review people offering that contribution, and Participants can browse openings that fit what they want to learn or build. Candidate suggestions organize this comparison but cannot establish interpersonal compatibility. The pilot should examine whether suggestions lead to relevant invitations and accepted placements, using participant feedback to detect misleading scores or systematically overlooked profiles.

**Discovery beyond existing contacts** is an opportunity to test, particularly for people entering an unfamiliar event community. The interdisciplinary hackathon research agenda identifies equitable participation and sociotechnical event design as important areas of study [10]. This supports treating access as a design responsibility, not claiming that software alone makes participation equitable. HackMatch's event-specific Looking-for-Team status lets a person express interest before having a team or knowing an owner. Browsable openings and declared objectives make it possible to seek learning-oriented collaborators without first entering an established friendship group. Responsive layouts and the specified accessibility target should make those tasks usable across different interaction needs. However, profile completion, language, confidence, and the number of active users may still affect who receives attention. A pilot should therefore include newcomers as well as experienced participants and examine unfilled openings, unsuccessful applicants, and task difficulties. The product's fictional personas guide those scenarios; they are not testimonials or substitutes for observing actual use.

**Trustworthy recruitment information** is another intended benefit. One approved event record connects official rules to all related teams, while the same membership check governs invitations and applications. An Administrator can correct a misleading event or disable an unsafe link without presenting a second copy as a new recruitment opportunity. These mechanisms are designed to reduce duplication and inconsistent commitments; the sources reviewed do not establish the incidence of fake listings or the reduction HackMatch will achieve. OWASP's authentication guidance supports layered controls such as throttling and secure session handling [11]. Canada's privacy principles emphasize limiting collection, limiting retention, and appropriate safeguards [12]. Those sources inform the risk model, rather than certify the implementation or establish that every legal regime applies. Restricting public profile exposure and keeping optional contact details within confirmed teams are concrete design choices. Operational evaluation should include review effort per event, erroneous approvals, correction handling, and privacy checks. Verification work has a cost, and that cost must be understood before the directory expands.

**Commercial value depends on proving these outcomes within a reachable community.** The initial adoption hypothesis is to recruit a pilot through campus clubs and hackathon communities around selected events, without assuming partnerships already exist. Enough active openings and candidates must be present at the same time for discovery to be useful. Funding would support MVP development, hosting and email delivery, verification/moderation work, and user evaluation; an amount and budget have not yet been approved. Participants are the primary users, while institutional or community support is a possible funding model to investigate, not a validated purchaser segment or a new Organizer account feature. The near-term investment decision should rest on usable team formation and sustainable service effort, not a percentage of GitHub's worldwide platform population [2]. Proposed evaluation would combine recruitment completion, user-reported clarity, repeat willingness to use the service, and operational cost per active event. Pricing, retention, revenue, and return on investment require evidence from that work. A focused pilot can establish whether further investment is justified without first committing to a broad event-management business.

### Similar Products

**Devpost and Devpost for Teams are direct competitive benchmarks.** Devpost's public hackathon participant page supports Looking-for-Team introductions and emailed team-up requests, followed by adding interested people to a project [13]. Devpost for Teams goes further: users can open a project for team building, specify desired roles and skills, browse available projects, and invite coworkers to accept or decline [14]. Its commercial offering supports private internal/customer hackathons, submissions, judging, reporting, and annual seat-based subscriptions [15]. These are substantial strengths, combining recruitment with an established event workflow and a defined business model. They also invalidate any claim that HackMatch invented structured teammate discovery. HackMatch's proposed position is a narrower participant-facing service around externally operated events, with explicit event verification and membership rules. Whether that focus justifies an additional tool must be tested against Devpost, not assumed. The reviewed sources do not establish comparable revenue, market share, or adoption of the individual team-building features; this proposal makes no such comparison.

**Major League Hacking (MLH) is an ecosystem substitute and potential discovery channel, rather than an identical application.** Its season programme connects student hackathons and promotes opportunities to participate, learn, and build. In its recap of the 2025 season, MLH reported reaching more than 150,000 hackers across 184 hackathons and other programmes, producing over 12,500 projects [16]. These are MLH's reported programme figures, not a count of unique customers available to HackMatch. Its event network and established community presence give participants an existing route to finding events and other builders. HackMatch overlaps with that discovery function, while proposing a persistent recruitment record for each approved event and an explicit path to a locked team. The cited programme description does not establish equivalent event-specific recruitment-state enforcement; that is a comparison of documented focus, not proof that no member event offers it. HackMatch should complement organizer-led activity where useful, without claiming an MLH relationship, replacing its community, or treating ecosystem participation as evidence of willingness to pay.

**Discord is a strong communication substitute with capabilities beyond a scrolling chat feed.** Its forum channels support persistent posts, tags, search, and permissions, allowing communities to organize recruitment discussions [7]. Discord reported more than 200 million monthly users in May 2025 [17], indicating substantial platform reach without revealing how many use it for hackathons. Existing communities and live conversation are advantages HackMatch's semester MVP will not reproduce. A well-managed server can already provide useful teammate introductions, and custom workflows may address additional needs. The remaining opportunity is narrower: forum posts alone are not the same product object as an event-specific vacancy with a shared approval and roster state. This is an inference from the documented forum model, not a claim that Discord cannot be configured or extended. HackMatch would need to demonstrate that maintaining recruitment decisions in a dedicated service outweighs the inconvenience of another account. Conversation can remain in the community's preferred tools, while HackMatch records the resulting membership commitments.

**LinkedIn is a broader professional-networking substitute.** Its official company statistics report more than 1.3 billion members at the time of this review and describe a global network with professional profiles, connections, learning, and job activity [18]. Membership is not monthly active usage, and the total should not be compared directly with Discord's monthly figure or MLH's programme reach. LinkedIn's strength for this use case is access to professional identities and contacts that can help someone discover a potential collaborator. Its documented business context is employment and professional opportunity, rather than the specific state of an opening on a hackathon team. HackMatch proposes to make that temporary intention explicit through event availability, vacancies, and joining decisions. This is a distinction in the workflow being evaluated, not evidence that LinkedIn users cannot organize teams successfully. A pilot should test whether people value this additional structure after they have found contacts, and whether maintaining another profile creates more effort than the focused recruitment process saves.

**Meetup is a substitute for community and event discovery.** Its official help documentation describes keyword search, event filters, and RSVPs that also join the host group [19]. Those functions support finding relevant communities and meeting people before a hackathon, with a clearer attendance workflow than an informal message thread. Bending Spoons identifies Meetup as an acquisition completed in January 2024 [20], providing evidence of corporate backing, although the reviewed materials do not provide standalone profitability or comparable recruitment revenue. The relevant distinction is between attending an event and joining a specific project team: HackMatch's target outcome is an accepted, capacity-checked membership followed by roster lock. Meetup's documented discovery flow centres on events and groups, so it may serve as a complementary introduction channel rather than a replacement for that narrower process. Across these five services, public metrics use different definitions and cannot support a market-share ranking. The competitive conclusion is that useful alternatives already exist; HackMatch must earn adoption through a demonstrably coherent workflow, not broad claims of competitor inadequacy.

### Differentiators

**First, HackMatch joins recruitment decisions into one event-specific membership workflow.** Ethnographic research on hackathon team formation examines how prospective teammates negotiate uncertain commitments [21]. A Participant can apply to a vacancy or accept an invitation, and both actions are governed by the same event eligibility, capacity, and one-confirmed-team rule. Team creation also confirms the owner's membership, so the restriction cannot be bypassed by owning a second team. The value for users is a shared answer to a practical question: is this person actually part of this team? Basic alerts and roster locking extend that answer from an initial request to a final commitment. Devpost already supports team-building and invitations [13], [14]; HackMatch therefore cannot defend itself through those individual features. Its proposed distinction is the particular integration of both joining routes, pre-lock changes, competing-request cancellation, and explicit roster finalization within a focused service. This is a design and execution proposition, not a verified superiority claim. The team must demonstrate it with realistic simultaneous joining attempts and observed user tasks. A technically consistent workflow is useful only if users understand its states and trust its decisions.

**Second, the event record is part of the recruitment control model.** Participants can propose missing events, but no public board or team formation is enabled until an Administrator approves the official event information. Duplicate detection and review aim to keep teams attached to the same event edition and rules. This matters because a valid roster depends on which hackathon it belongs to, including that event's published team limits. It also gives users a defined route for correcting an inaccurate record. Devpost for Teams provides a wider organizer-controlled event platform, while MLH provides event discovery [15], [16]. HackMatch's intended position is a participant-submitted, administrator-verified directory connected directly to team formation without operating the event itself. The comparison does not establish exclusive verification technology. The potential advantage lies in reliable review practices, useful coverage, and a consistent experience when an event changes or duplicates appear. Human review can also become a bottleneck. Before scaling, the team must establish review effort, decision quality, and a correction process that users can understand; a verified label alone is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness.

**Third, candidate suggestions are explicit decision aids for Team Leads.** For a concrete example of the need for teammate discovery, Imperial College London's Hong Kong and Macau hackathon guidance leaves finding teammates to participants and states that organizers do not facilitate matching [22]. This illustrates one relevant setting, not a market-wide gap. The MVP compares a vacancy's requirements with available Participants' self-declared skills, goals, and commitment, and shows why a candidate is relevant. A lead looking for design support should be able to connect a suggestion to that opening, rather than infer that the person received a universal talent rating. Participants retain a separate route to opportunity by browsing and applying themselves. Devpost for Teams documents roles and desired skills in team building [14], so skill-based recruitment is not exclusive to HackMatch. The proposed value is the combination of visible matching factors, event availability, and the lead's final judgment. It can be evaluated by asking whether suggestions are understandable and lead to suitable conversations or placements. The current numerical scoring policy requires calibration and team confirmation; no probability of success or bias-free ranking is claimed. Incomplete profiles and overstated skills remain limitations. Advanced AI, participant-facing recommendation algorithms, and automated decisions about who must join a team are not part of this MVP proposition.

**Defensibility must be earned through execution and evidence.** The evidence reviewed does not establish these features as barriers to replication by established competitors, and HackMatch currently has no demonstrated proprietary technology, exclusive partnership, or durable data advantage. The plausible longer-term advantage would come from reliable event curation, an active community with relevant openings, and recruitment workflows improved through responsible user evaluation. Those are potential assets, not present achievements. Existing products also benefit from audiences and established habits, making the extra account and profile a material adoption risk. A sensible investment gate is therefore to complete the Must-Have product and run a bounded evaluation against the alternatives participants actually use. The evaluation should examine recruitment completion and comprehension alongside administration effort, accessibility difficulties, and reasons for abandonment. The team should approve its cohort, measurement method, and success thresholds before interpreting results. Security assurance also needs care: the mandated email OTP flow is not a claim of NIST-compliant authentication [5]. Funding and launch discussions must retain that limitation and the unresolved policy-register items. The investment sought is staged support to demonstrate reliable recruitment, validate adoption, and establish credible operating costs before expanding the product.
